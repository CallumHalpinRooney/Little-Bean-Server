## 1. Choosing a target date, and why "10th–12th century" needs narrowing

The standing buildings weren't all built at once. A single date where *everything is complete and roofed* never quite existed:

| Building | Best current dating | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Cathedral nave (first phase, with antae) | 10th or 11th c., enlarged c. late 11th/early 12th c. | [E] fabric; date [I] |
| Round Tower | "around the turn of the 12th century" (NMS) | [I] from style/parallels |
| St Mary's Church (nave) | Early; lintelled west door. Often put 10th c. or earlier | [I] |
| Reefert Church | c. 1100 | [I] |
| St Kevin's Church ("Kitchen"), nave | c. 1100 or a little before | [I] |
| St Kevin's Church, chancel + north sacristy | Soon after the nave, 12th c. Chancel **demolished c. 1800** | [E] footing survives; date [I] |
| St Kevin's Church, round belfry | 12th c. | [I] |
| Priest's House | mid-12th c. Romanesque | [I] from style |
| St Saviour's (1.5 km east) | 1150s, linked to Laurence O'Toole | [I] with a strong tradition |
| Cathedral chancel + sacristy | **late 12th / early 13th c.** | [I] from style |

**Recommendation: set the reconstruction at c. 1170–1200.** That is the only window where the cathedral chancel plausibly exists *and* the settlement still has its status. Glendalough stopped being a separate diocese in 1214–16, and the NMS notes that "by 1200 its status and importance went into rapid decline". Laurence O'Toole was abbot from 1153 and became Archbishop of Dublin in 1162. The annals record the place "plundered by the foreigners" in 1176.

If you prefer an **earlier date (c. 1150)**, show the cathedral as the nave alone, perhaps with a smaller, lost eastern chancel [S], and drop the Priest's House. Either way, state the date on screen. "10th–12th century" as a single image is a composite that never existed.

## 2. The structures

### 2.1 Round Tower

- **Fabric [E]:** Mica-schist rubble with granite dressings. About **30–30.5 m** high. Doorway about **3.5 m** above ground level, which is standard for Irish round towers. Originally about **six storeys** of timber floors, reached by ladders. The top storey has windows facing roughly the four cardinal points *(verify the window count against Leask or Barrow)*.
- **Conical cap [E, with a caveat]:** Rebuilt in **1876** from the original stones found inside the tower. The shape and material are probably close to authentic, but the exact profile is a 19th-century rebuild.
- **Purpose [I, well supported]:** The Irish name is *cloicthech* ("bell-house"). It was a belfry, most likely for a hand-bell rung from the top windows rather than a hung bell. It also served as a treasury and occasional refuge. It was a status monument too: towers cluster at the major churches. O'Keeffe (2004) and Ó Carragáin (2010) stress their role in liturgy and display, beyond the older "refuge from Vikings" story.
- **Appearance at the peak:** Essentially as today, but with a timber door and frame at the raised doorway, and a movable ladder or timber stair to reach it [I]. Whether the exterior was **lime-rendered or limewashed** is **unknown** [S]. Depict bare stone as the conservative option.

### 2.2 The Cathedral (Sts Peter and Paul)

- **Phasing [E]:** The **nave** is the oldest part: a large single-cell church with **antae**, where the side walls project beyond the gable walls. The lower courses use very large, squared mica-schist blocks, possibly reused from an earlier stone church. The **chancel** (with a Romanesque chancel arch and east window) and a **sacristy** on the south side of the chancel were added in the **late 12th / early 13th century**. The **west doorway** is lintelled with a relieving arch. Ambry and piscina survive in the chancel.
- **Scale [E]:** It is one of the largest churches built in early medieval Ireland (NMS). The internal nave is on the order of **15 × 9 m** *(verify against Leask 1955 or the OPW plan)*.
- **Roof [I]:** A 9 m span can't take a corbelled stone roof. It must have had a **steep-pitched timber roof**. Irish pre-Norman church gables are typically steep, around 55–60°, and the surviving gable fabric should be used to set the pitch. Ó Carragáin and others read the antae as a skeuomorph, a stone copy of the corner posts of timber churches.
- **Roof covering [S, bounded by evidence]:** The main candidates are **oak shingles** (Irish *slinn*, attested in early Irish texts, with a shingled church shown in the Book of Kells Temptation page) or **thatch**. Lead is recorded for Armagh's oratory in 1008, so it wasn't unknown, but it was exceptional. **Recommendation: oak shingles weathered silver-grey.** Flag this as a choice.
- **Finish [S]:** Walls may have been limewashed or rendered, and the interior plastered and painted. There is no Glendalough-specific evidence. Keep the render subtle or leave the stone bare, and flag it.

### 2.3 St Kevin's Church ("St Kevin's Kitchen")

- **Fabric [E]:** A small barrel-vaulted oratory of hard mica schist. It carries a **steep corbelled stone roof** over an internal stone vault, with a **croft** (loft) between the two. It is one of the few roofed early medieval churches in Ireland, and the only roofed building at Glendalough. The closest parallel is St Columb's House, Kells.
- **Phasing [E/I]:**
  1. A plain rectangular nave, **c. 1100 or shortly before** [I].
  2. A chancel arch cut through the east wall, with a **small stone-roofed chancel** and an **interconnecting sacristy to its north** [E]. The chancel was **demolished c. 1800** and only its footing and the arch remain [E]. **Your reconstruction must restore the chancel.** At the peak, the building was not the single block you see today.
  3. A **round belfry** with a conical cap and four small top windows, rising from the west end of the roof, 12th c. [I]. The "kitchen" nickname comes from the belfry looking like a chimney.
- **Appearance at the peak:** Grey stone roof of overlapping slabs, the belfry, the chancel and sacristy stepping down to the east, all in the same stone. The chancel's roof pitch and height are **inferred** from the surviving sacristy and the scar of the arch [I].

### 2.4 The Priest's House

- **Fabric [E, heavily qualified]:** A tiny Romanesque building, mid-12th c. It was **almost entirely rebuilt by the Board of Works in the 1870s** from fallen stones, following **Gabriel Beranger's 1779 drawing**. It has a decorated Romanesque arch on the east side and a carved doorway with a badly worn figured lintel. **Much of what stands today is a Victorian reading of an 18th-century sketch.**
- **Purpose [S]:** The name comes from its use for burying priests in the 18th–19th centuries, not from any medieval function. A frequent and plausible suggestion is that it was a **shrine or mortuary house for St Kevin's relics** [S, but it fits 12th-century practice; see O'Keeffe 2003].
- **Roof [S]:** Unknown. The span is small enough for a stone roof like St Kevin's, or for timber and shingle. **Recommendation: stone roof, flagged**, because it echoes the high-status stone-roofed shrine-chapels of the period. Either choice is defensible.

### 2.5 St Kieran's Church

- **Fabric [E]:** Only **low foundations of a nave-and-chancel church** survive, **uncovered in 1875**, near the river on the low ground by St Kevin's Church.
- **Dedication [I]:** To Ciarán of Clonmacnoise, which may point to a Clonmacnoise interest or property at Glendalough.
- **Excavation next door [E]:** In 2011, 40 m south of the church at a ford over the Glenealo River, O'Donovan found a granite riverbank revetment, an earthen bank (either an **early enclosure around St Kieran's or part of a mill**, still unresolved), and a 13th–15th-c. paved path (Excavations.ie 2011).
- **Superstructure [S]:** Everything above the foundations is guesswork. **Recommendation:** a small rubble church with a timber and shingle roof, simpler than its neighbours.

### 2.6 St Mary's Church (Our Lady's)

- **Fabric [E]:** Stands **west of the main cluster**. A well-built early nave with a granite **west doorway** that has inclined jambs and a massive lintel, carved with a saltire cross on its underside. A chancel was added later.
- **Tradition [S]:** Associated with a nunnery and, in some traditions, with Kevin's burial. Treat both as tradition.
- **At the peak:** Roofed in timber and shingle [I/S]. It may sit inside or outside the main enclosure, which is unresolved (see §2.8).

### 2.7 The Gateway

- **Fabric [E]:** The **only surviving pre-Norman monastic gatehouse in Ireland**. It is a square building with **two round granite arches**, one at each end of the passage, and antae on the north and south walls. **Corbels inside show there was an upper floor.** Some of the **paving of the causeway** through it survives.
- **Sanctuary stone [E]:** A **cross-inscribed stone** in the passage wall marks the edge of the sanctuary, the protected area of legal refuge (*termonn*).
- **Roof [I]:** The antae imply a **timber roof**. Its form is unknown, so a steep gabled shingle roof is the consistent choice [S].
- **At the peak:** Two storeys, a timber door or gates in the arches [I], and a paved causeway continuing west into the core [I, extrapolated from the surviving paving].

### 2.8 St Kevin's Cross, the graveyard and the enclosure

- **St Kevin's Cross [E]:** A plain granite high cross with an unpierced ring, near the cathedral. It was probably in place at the peak [I].
- **Enclosure [E, contested]:** Geophysics (Elliott 2011, extended by the Discovery Programme in 2015) and UCD / Irish Archaeology Field School excavation (2014–17) in the field beside the core found **seven or more activity phases from the 7th to the 14th century**, with possible industrial features (kilns) and rectangular buildings. The most striking find is a **ditch up to 8 m wide**. It was first read as the monastic vallum, but medieval pottery in its lowest fills means it is **unlikely to be much earlier than the 12th century**. **The full line of the early monastic enclosure is not established.**
- **Graveyard [E/I]:** Today's graveyard is full of 18th–20th-c. headstones, which **must be removed** from the reconstruction. Medieval burial was dense and repeated. Above ground it showed as **low grassed mounds, small cross-inscribed slabs and recumbent cross-slabs** [I, from the carved stones found on site and parallels such as Clonmacnoise], not rows of upright headstones.

### 2.9 Beyond the main cluster (usually visible up-valley in aerial views)

- **Reefert Church** (c. 1100), near the Upper Lake. The name is *Ríg-ferta*, "burial place of kings", traditionally of the Uí Tuathail (O'Toole) kings [E for the name; I for the use].
- **Templenaskellig**, St Kevin's Bed (an artificial cave) and St Kevin's Cell, all by the **Upper Lake**. This is the hermitage side of the valley.
- **Trinity Church**, on the approach road east of the gateway. It had a west annex with a round belfry that **fell in a storm in 1818** [E], so restore it if it is in frame.
- **St Saviour's**, 1.5 km east. A 12th-c. Romanesque church with some of the finest carving in Ireland, restored in the 1870s. It is probably out of frame.

> **Geography note:** The main monastic cluster lies at the **east end of the valley, by the Lower Lake**, where the Glendasan and Glenealo rivers meet. The Upper Lake is about 1.5 km further west. If your photo shows the Upper Lake close by, it's probably taken from a different spot, or the cluster in it is Reefert rather than the main site.

## 3. Monastic life at Glendalough, c. 1100–1200

**It wasn't a "monastery" in the Benedictine sense.** It was a major **church settlement**. Contemporaries could call it a *civitas*. Colmán Etchingham (in Doherty et al. 2011) argues that such places held several kinds of people at once:

- a clerical and monastic elite: abbot, bishop, *fer léigind* (head of the school), priests and anchorites;
- ascetic hermits at the Upper Lake;
- and, in the largest numbers, **manaig**: lay tenant families who farmed church land, owed dues and labour, and were buried in church ground.

The "monks praying and farming" picture merges those groups into one.

**The abbacy was run by powerful families.** Ailbhe Mac Shamhráin (1996) traces how control shifted between Leinster dynasties, ending with the **Uí Muiredaig / Uí Tuathail (O'Tooles)**. Laurence O'Toole (Lorcán Ua Tuathail) was the son of a local king. As a boy he was a hostage of Diarmait Mac Murchada, King of Leinster, who later married Laurence's sister. That is the same Diarmait whose invitation brought the Anglo-Normans in 1169. So in the 1160s–70s Glendalough sat at the centre of the politics that ended pre-Norman Ireland. Kings being buried at Reefert fits this dynastic-church picture.

**It was a diocese, and then it wasn't.** The Synod of Ráith Bressail (1111) made Glendalough one of the two dioceses of north Leinster. It was absorbed into Dublin in 1214–16. Your chosen date sits in the last generations of Glendalough as a bishop's seat, which is why the cathedral was enlarged when it was.

**Population [S]: nobody knows.** There are no census-type sources. Scale can be argued from the size of the church complex, the extent of the geophysics anomalies, and parallels such as Clonmacnoise. A settlement of **a few hundred**, rising sharply on feast days, is a defensible order of magnitude. It is **not** a documented figure, so don't put a number on screen.

**A working settlement.** The kilns and rectangular buildings from the geophysics, charcoal-rich ditch fills, and a mill imply grain drying, metalworking and milling on the spot [E/I]. The mill is suggested by the 2011 excavation and by an annal entry *(verify: AFM s.a. 1177 records a flood at Glendalough that carried away a bridge and a mill; check the exact wording on CELT)*. The valley's oak woodland was a resource worth exploiting. Tree-ring analysis ties the Skuldelev 2 longship, built in Dublin c. 1042, to oak from the Dublin region, and some writers place that source at or near Glendalough [I, contested].

**Daily rhythm [I].** The clerical day followed the canonical hours: night office, then prayer at intervals through the day, marked by the hand-bell from the tower and from the belfry of St Kevin's. Around that ran a school (Laurence was educated here), book production (the manuscript Oxford, Bodleian Rawlinson B 502 has been argued, contestedly, to include the lost "Book of Glendalough", c. 1130s), property administration, hospitality for guests, and the lay agricultural year.

**Pilgrimage made the space into a route [E/I].** Kevin's cult brought pilgrims:

- along **St Kevin's Road** over the Wicklow Gap from Hollywood (Price 1940);
- to **Trinity Church** on the approach;
- through the **gateway**, past the **sanctuary stone**, into protected ground;
- to the **graves and relic-churches** of the core;
- and then **west up the valley** to Reefert and the Upper Lake hermitage (St Kevin's Bed, Templenaskellig), with cross-marked "stations" along the way.

The layout reads as a **graded journey**, from the busy lay settlement at the gate, through the sacred burial core, to the ascetic wilderness at the far end. The "seven visits to Glendalough equal one to Rome" saying is **later tradition** [S], so don't attribute it to the 12th century.

**What the graveyard tells us.** People were buried **as close as possible to the saint** (*ad sanctos*). Where you were buried marked status: kings at Reefert, clergy and patrons near the relic-churches, tenant families in the wider church ground. The Priest's House, if it was Kevin's shrine, would have been the magnet for this. The graveyard was not a separate "cemetery zone". It was the processional and ritual heart of the site, with the round tower rising from it.

## 4. Image-generation prompts

### 4.1 Practical advice first (it matters more than the wording)

A text prompt alone **won't reproduce a specific drone vantage point**. To get "this view, restored":

1. **SDXL / Flux + ControlNet** (depth + canny or MLSD) using the drone photo as the control image, at a control weight of about 0.6–0.8. This fixes the camera, the terrain and the building footprints. The prompt then only has to supply materials, roofs and atmosphere.
2. **Inpaint** the specific additions: roofs, St Kevin's chancel, the gateway's upper storey, lay houses. Also inpaint *out* the modern features: headstones, car parks, the visitor centre, the hotel, tarmac paths, fences, the 1870s walls around St Kieran's, and conifer plantations.
3. **Midjourney:** use the drone photo as an image prompt (`--iw 1.5–2`) and `--style raw`. Expect some geometry drift, and fix it in a paint-over.
4. For AR, the stronger long-term route is a **3D model** built from photogrammetry of the ruins, with roofs added from these specs and the renders used as look-dev. Say if you want that brief.
